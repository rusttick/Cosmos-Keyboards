# Fundamentals: RSI, hand kinematics, and what our scan model can and can't tell us

This document sets up the problem before we design any placement algorithm. It covers three things: (1) why key placement matters for injury risk at all, in terms a kinematic model can act on; (2) exactly what our `/scan` pipeline measures and doesn't measure, so we know what the model is actually capable of grounding; (3) the theoretical solution space for "ergonomic" key placement relative to a kinematic model, with construction constraints (case walls, keycap geometry, manufacturability) set aside.

## 1. What repetitive strain injury actually depends on, in terms we can model

RSI/RSI-adjacent conditions (carpal tunnel syndrome, tendinitis, cumulative trauma disorder) are not caused by typing itself — they're caused by a small set of measurable biomechanical risk factors compounding over many repetitions: **force, repetition, awkward/non-neutral posture, and sustained static load**, per NIOSH's ergonomics framework and the general RSI literature ([Wikipedia: Repetitive strain injury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetitive_strain_injury); [Ergodyne](https://www.ergodyne.com/blog/repetitive-strain-injuries-in-workplace-types-causes-prevention)). Of these four, key _placement_ (as opposed to switch choice, typing technique, or work/rest scheduling) is really only able to act on **posture** — specifically, how far each joint has to deviate from its neutral/relaxed angle to reach a given key, and how much _that deviation varies_ key-to-key.

This reframes what our kinematic hand model needs to be useful for: it doesn't need to predict injury outright, it needs to answer _"for candidate key position P, how far is each joint in the chain from its own neutral resting angle, and is that deviation within a comfortable sub-range of that joint's total ROM?"_ — a query the model (bone lengths + joint axes + ROM) is structurally suited to answer, once ROM is added (which today it isn't — see §3).

The clearest domain-specific evidence that posture (not force or key feel) is the lever ergonomic _keyboard geometry_ actually pulls: split/tented keyboard studies show a direct, measurable posture effect from geometry alone.

- Split keyboards reduce mean wrist ulnar deviation from ~12° to within 5° of neutral compared to a standard keyboard, and increase the fraction of typing time spent within a defined "neutral zone" of wrist motion from 44%→71% (flexion/extension) and 25%→78% (radial/ulnar) ([Wrist and forearm posture from typing on split and vertically inclined computer keyboards, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10774127/); [Effects of split keyboard geometry on upper body postures, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19308823/)).
- A slant/tenting angle of 10°–12.5°, or half-separation to roughly shoulder width, is reported as the geometry that best achieves near-neutral radial/ulnar wrist deviation ([Design Features of Alternative Computer Keyboards, JOSPT](https://www.jospt.org/doi/pdf/10.2519/jospt.2004.34.10.638)).

That's all _wrist_-level evidence, because it's the easiest joint to instrument and the literature concentrates there. It generalizes downward, though: the same logic (minimize sustained deviation from a joint's neutral angle, minimize the _range_ a joint has to sweep repeatedly) applies to MCP/PIP/DIP flexion and thumb CMC/MCP motion — it's just less studied because finger-joint-level keyboard ergonomics research is thin. This is a real gap: **most of the wrist-posture literature does not transfer numerically to finger-joint placement, only structurally** (the "minimize deviation from neutral, minimize per-key swept range" principle carries over; the specific angle targets do not).

## 2. What the scan pipeline actually produces

Walking the pipeline as implemented in this repo (`/scan`, `src/routes/scan/lib/hand.ts`, `src/lib/hand.ts`):

1. **Capture.** MediaPipe Hands runs on a monocular RGB video (webcam or, per this project's workaround, a phone-recorded file played back through a `<video>` element), producing 21 hand landmarks per frame, per hand, in two forms: 2D image-plane landmarks and `keypoints3D` — MediaPipe's own learned "world landmark" estimate, in meters, roughly hand-scale.
2. **Per-frame bone vectors.** Each frame's 21 landmarks are collapsed into 5 finger chains of 4 bone-direction vectors each (wrist→MCP, MCP→PIP, PIP→DIP, DIP→tip), re-expressed in a standardized hand-local basis computed from the palm's own geometry (`makeBasis` in `hand.ts`), so all frames are comparable regardless of how the hand was rotated in front of the camera.
3. **Aggregation across the whole recording.** Over ~1000 accepted frames per hand (frames below a 0.7 confidence score are dropped), the pipeline computes, per bone: an _average length_ (`stats.ts`), and per joint, an _average orientation_ fit via SVD/PCA across all pooled frame samples (`fitNorms`/`averageNorms` in `hand.ts`) — the dominant principal component of the observed bone-direction spread becomes that joint's rotation axis.
4. **Output.** A `Joints` object: for each finger, 4 joints, each with a bone `length` and a local rotation-axis frame (`V`/`Vinv`), plus a fixed DOF count (0/1/2) assigned by an anatomical _assumption_, not measured from the data.

### Strengths

- **Bone lengths are well-measured.** Averaging ~1000 frames of a purpose-built network's per-frame regression gives a low-variance length estimate — this is the part of the model most trustworthy on its face.
- **Joint axis _orientation_ is data-derived, not guessed.** Fitting the rotation axis via PCA over real observed motion (rather than assuming, say, "MCP flexes purely about the palm-normal axis") means the model reflects how _this specific hand_ actually articulates, including any consistent anatomical skew.
- **Self-consistent cross-finger geometry.** Because every finger's base joint (wrist→MCP) is captured in the same standardized hand frame, the relative offsets between fingers (palm width, finger-base stagger) come out correct automatically without needing separate measurement — see the earlier finding that this is exactly what feeds finger-cluster auto-stagger today.

### Weaknesses

- **No range-of-motion data at all.** Averaging pools every frame together; nothing in the pipeline records _how far_ a joint swung, only its _mean_ observed direction. The one ROM-like number in the codebase (`MAX_PAN`, 30°/40°) is a hardcoded global constant, not derived from any individual's scan.
- **Absolute scale is unreliable for the simple single-camera scan path.** A monocular RGB view is fundamentally scale-ambiguous — a larger hand farther from the camera is visually indistinguishable from a smaller hand closer up — so MediaPipe's `keypoints3D` falls back on a learned average-hand-size prior for that one degree of freedom. This is a known, general limitation of monocular 3D hand pose estimation, not specific to this codebase ("recovering absolute (camera-space) hand pose from a single RGB image remains challenging due to depth ambiguity," per recent monocular hand-pose work ([Monocular 3D Hand Pose Estimation with Implicit Camera Alignment, arXiv 2506.11133](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.11133))). _Relative_ bone-length ratios are comparatively reliable, since that's what the network is actually trained to regress per-frame; only the single global scale factor is in question — which is why caliper-based scale calibration (a single correction factor) is a sound fix, discussed elsewhere in this project's notes.
- **Depth/yaw ambiguity and self-occlusion degrade specific joints unevenly.** Monocular estimation is known to have larger errors on out-of-plane (yaw/depth) motion and during self-occlusion or simultaneous multi-joint motion ([Hand tracking for clinical applications: validation of GMH/GMH-D, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1746809424005664)) — the thumb, which frequently occludes itself against the palm or other fingers during natural motion, is disproportionately exposed to this.
- **The thumb's CMC joint is modeled as fixed (0 DOF).** This is a deliberate simplification stated in the code's own docstring, not a bug — but it means the model cannot express true thumb opposition (the CMC saddle joint is exactly where opposition's rotation+abduction actually happens, per the biomechanics literature: "motions at the CMC joint occur ... primarily in 2 degrees of freedom ... [with] flexion-extension total range of motion of 40°-50°," and opposition further couples CMC flexion with pronation and with MCP flexion ([Anatomy and Biomechanics of the Thumb Carpometacarpal Joint, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S104866661730109X); [Influence of age and gender on normative passive ROM of the thumb CMC joint, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0894113017300595)); real CMC opposition is 6-DOF-capable at the joint surface, of which only 2 are typically exercised functionally. Fixing it at 0 DOF here means the thumb's reachable workspace, whatever we compute, is a cone anchored at the scanned CMC orientation — not a genuine opposition arc.
- **DOF assignment is an assumption, not a fit.** The pipeline decides in advance which joints get 1 vs. 2 rotational degrees of freedom (per the code's documented finger-vs-thumb rule); it never tests whether the observed motion actually supports that assumption for a given individual's hand.

## 3. The solution space for ergonomic key placement, unconstrained by physical build

Setting aside case walls, keycap manufacturability, and connector routing, "ergonomic key placement" reduces to a joint-cost-minimization problem layered on the kinematic model:

**The core principle, grounded in motor control literature, not just intuition:** biological reaching/pointing movements are organized by the nervous system to minimize a smoothness/effort cost, not to move through arbitrary paths. Flash & Hogan's minimum-jerk model — since replicated and extended by minimum-torque-change and minimum-energy formulations — shows that natural reaching trajectories are the ones that minimize jerk (or torque-change, or energy) integrated over the movement, producing the characteristic straight-line, bell-shaped-velocity paths observed in real reaching data ([A simple control policy for achieving minimum jerk trajectories, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0893608011002978); general summary of minimum-jerk/minimum-torque-change/minimum-energy cost functionals in biological motion). The direct implication for key placement: **a key that a finger can reach via a short, low-curvature, low-joint-excursion path from its resting posture is intrinsically "cheaper" to press, repeatedly, than one requiring a longer or more convoluted path — independent of any subjective comfort judgment.** This gives a principled, computable proxy for "ergonomic," rather than one built on guessed curvature constants: for a candidate key position, integrate joint-angle change (or, more simply, deviation from neutral rest angle) along the FK path from rest posture to that position, and prefer lower-cost placements.

This suggests three concrete, model-computable properties that unconstrained ergonomic placement should optimize for, each directly addressable by the joint data we have (once ROM is added):

1. **Minimize deviation from each joint's neutral/resting angle**, not just "stay within ROM." A key reachable only near the _extreme_ of a joint's range is more costly than one reachable near mid-range, even if both are technically "in range" — this is the same logic the split-keyboard wrist studies demonstrate empirically (time spent near neutral, not just time spent in-bounds, is the metric that correlates with reduced strain risk). Practically: score candidate key positions by (say) the sum of squared joint-angle deviations from rest, not a binary reachability check.
2. **Minimize the _range_ any single joint has to sweep across the full set of keys assigned to that finger.** If a finger's keys are spread across its full ROM, every keystroke risks landing near an extreme; if they're clustered near a comfortable mid-range band, no keystroke does. This directly motivates the earlier proposal to expose "% of ROM used" as a tunable band per finger rather than trying to auto-maximize key count — it's the placement-time analogue of the neutral-zone metric from the wrist literature.
3. **Account for finger interdependence (enslaving), not just per-finger independent ROM.** Fingers are not independently actuated — pressing with one finger measurably co-activates neighboring fingers via shared multi-tendon muscles and cross-finger connective tissue, an effect that's large, roughly symmetric, and distance-dependent (stronger between adjacent fingers) ([Variable and Asymmetric Range of Enslaving, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0168636)); "no finger can move independently through the full range of finger flexion, although some degree of full independence is present for smaller movements." Index finger is the most independent; ring is typically least ([Finger inter-dependence, PSU thesis](https://etda.libraries.psu.edu/files/final_submissions/5964)). This has a direct placement implication beyond simple keycap-collision avoidance: **placing two different fingers' keys such that pressing one requires a posture near where the other finger's enslaved motion would also want to go is a hidden ergonomic cost**, distinct from and not captured by pure geometric non-overlap. It also grounds "stronger finger preference cutting volume from weaker fingers" in real biomechanics, rather than treating it as an arbitrary weighting knob: the weaker/more-enslaved fingers (ring, pinky) have a _narrower_ independently-controllable sub-range in practice than their raw anatomical ROM suggests, so their usable placement volume should be discounted more than their raw ROM alone would imply.

**What this does _not_ resolve, and what remains genuinely open once construction constraints are reintroduced:** none of the above tells us how many keys a finger _should_ get, only how to rank candidate positions once a count is chosen — count vs. comfort is a tradeoff the literature doesn't settle for us (more keys per finger means, all else equal, some keys land farther from neutral). Similarly, key _travel/force_ is a separate, largely orthogonal risk lever from _placement_ — shorter-travel switches are reported to reduce finger flexion/extension range and peak muscular strain per keystroke ([Design Features of Alternative Computer Keyboards, JOSPT](https://www.jospt.org/doi/pdf/10.2519/jospt.2004.34.10.638); general low-profile-switch ergonomics claims are common in industry sources but are not backed by the same quality of peer-reviewed evidence as the split-keyboard wrist studies above, and should be treated with more skepticism until better-sourced) — but that's a hardware choice this project already treats independently of geometry, and should stay that way.

## 4. Tenting: informed manual placement, not a scanned measurement

Today's tenting parameter (`c.curvature.tenting`, `config.ts:871`) is a single scalar rotation angle applied to an otherwise flat key-well plane — the shell is a plane, tenting rotates it about one axis, done. It's worth naming explicitly what that model assumes and doesn't: it assumes the _only_ board-orientation adjustment worth making is a single-axis tilt, chosen with no reference to the user's own anatomy at all.

An earlier version of this section proposed fixing that by adding a scanned wrist joint to the kinematic model and deriving tenting from the user's measured neutral wrist posture. That doesn't hold up: reliably constraining a forearm well enough to measure wrist articulation turns out to require something close to skeletal fixation (see `scanning.md`'s wrist section for the full reasoning) — not achievable with a desk fixture, and MediaPipe Hands has no forearm/elbow landmark to measure against even if it were. Wrist posture isn't something this scan pipeline can reliably produce, so it shouldn't be posed as scanned input to tenting.

**What's actually achievable, and still a real improvement over today:** tenting becomes an **informed manual placement** rather than either a blind angle guess (today) or an (unreliable) automated measurement. The user positions the whole hand assembly — built from the _reliably_ scanned finger data — against the virtual keyboard surface interactively, via `SolvedHand.position` made UI-adjustable (already a free 6-DOF anchor in the codebase, just not exposed as one today). The wrist-posture literature doesn't go to waste here; it moves from "calibration target for a measurement we can't actually take" to **live guardrail feedback during that manual placement** — surfacing the implied wrist extension/deviation angle as the user drags the hand into position, and flagging it against the same evidence-backed thresholds (extension beyond ~30°, radial deviation beyond ~15°, tied to measurably increased carpal tunnel pressure — [Effect of Wrist Posture on Carpal Tunnel Pressure While Typing](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18383144/)). Flexion-extension and radial-ulnar deviation are coupled, not independent ([Coupling between wrist flexion-extension and radial-ulnar deviation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15621323/)), so the guardrail should reflect that interaction rather than bounding each axis separately.

This is a smaller claim than the original proposal, and an honest one: the system isn't measuring the user's wrist, it's helping them _judge_ a placement decision they're still making themselves — informed by real thresholds instead of guessing blind, but not pretending to a precision the scan pipeline can't deliver.
