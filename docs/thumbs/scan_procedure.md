# Scan procedure: a capture protocol designed around MediaPipe Hands' actual failure modes

This is the process document: for each capture, what position the hands are in, what's said to the user, what's recorded, what's calculated from it, and how the software knows a capture is done well enough to move on. Implementation details — data structures, module boundaries, storage — belong in `scan3.md`, kept separate from process.

The organizing constraint behind every decision below: MediaPipe Hands is not uniformly reliable across viewing angles. Its two failure modes are different in kind, and the protocol has to answer them differently:

- **A systematic, not-just-noisy bias when the dorsal (back-of-hand) side faces the camera.** This is a documented, reproducible MediaPipe limitation — world-landmark positions (especially MCPs) collapse toward a degenerate, flattened configuration specifically in dorsal view, across different lighting, poses, and hands ([github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe#5156](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe/issues/5156), itself a duplicate of an earlier report (#3994), meaning the same failure has persisted across multiple MediaPipe releases years apart with no fix). Pooling/averaging frames — which is how this pipeline turns per-frame observations into bone lengths and joint axes — cannot avarge away a systematic per-orientation bias; it only tightens an estimate around whatever population of frames it's fed. So the fix has to be upstream of the fit: **never feed dorsal-facing frames into a quantitative fit at all.** The mechanism is believed (not officially confirmed by Google) to be training-data asymmetry — palm images carry far stronger depth cues (creases, nail/knuckle texture, finger separation) than the back of the hand does — rather than a projective-geometry effect, since a hand's dorsal and palmar views are geometrically symmetric with respect to any single joint's rotation axis.
- **Ordinary foreshortening and self-occlusion**, which is a real, continuous-valued, camera-angle-dependent effect (not specific to MediaPipe, not a bug) — the closer a bone's rotation axis lies to the camera's own view direction, the less of that rotation is resolved by the 2D image and the more the estimate depends on unreliable monocular depth. This is a matter of degree, and can be worked around by choosing which of the (dorsal-excluded) camera-hand orientations best exposes a given motion, and by keeping fingers spread and unoccluded from one another.

Everything below follows from treating these as two different problems: the first is solved by a hard rule (no dorsal-facing capture, ever), the second by choosing orientation per motion.

## Camera and hand orientation

The camera sits fixed on the floor (or a low stand at floor height), facing straight up, with nothing else — no desk, no wall, no other object — in its field of view. Both hands are held freely in the space above it, unsupported, at a comfortable separation (roughly typing width, so a bilateral capture never has one hand occluding the other).

This single placement, together with restricting all hand motion to the range between **palm facing the camera** and **palms facing each other**, is enough to keep every capture inside MediaPipe's reliable regime without ever needing to discover or compensate for an unknown camera angle:

- **Palms down** (facing the floor-mounted, upward-looking camera) presents the palmar surface directly to the lens — the well-trained, well-textured case.
- **Palms facing each other** (each hand rotated roughly a quarter turn from palms-down, as in a tented/split-keyboard hand posture) presents the radial or ulnar edge of the hand to the camera — a lateral/profile view, useful for motions that foreshorten badly face-on.
- Sweeping between these two postures is exactly the pronation-to-neutral range a tented or split keyboard actually asks the hands to move through, so the orientations this protocol needs are also the ones a real typing posture produces — no artificial gesture required to reach them.
- Rotating a further quarter turn past palms-facing-each-other reaches palms-up, i.e. the dorsal side facing the camera. **That configuration is never used for capture.** The whole working range this protocol asks for stops a quarter turn short of it on either side.

Deviating from these two canonical orientations is permitted, but only when doing so measurably reduces occlusion or foreshortening for a specific motion (the thumb's self-occlusion against the palm is the recurring example below) — never as a way to reach a more "natural"-looking pose that happens to expose the dorsal side.

Because camera placement and the working hand-orientation range are now prescribed rather than arbitrary, no per-session gesture is needed to discover the camera's geometric relationship to the hand — an earlier design considered a three-finger orthogonal calibration pose for exactly this purpose, but it existed only to compensate for camera placement being unconstrained; fixing the placement removes the need for it.

Every capture below shares one baseline quality gate: reject frames below MediaPipe's own 0.7 confidence threshold; if a segment's accepted-frame yield is too low, it fails its completion check regardless of any capture-specific criterion, and is retried.

## Caliper calibration

Done once, offline, no camera involved — orientation and camera placement are irrelevant here.

Measure the middle finger (longest, so measurement-noise-to-length ratio is smallest), fully extended and flat, calipers along the finger's length axis (never pinching girth/thickness, which measures soft tissue rather than skeletal length). The two landmark points are the fingertip pad center (matching where MediaPipe places the tip landmark, not the nail's edge) and the centroid of the base-crease cluster (the flexion crease dividing finger from palm is usually several close, non-identical lines — MediaPipe's own "joint" landmarks were trained against visually annotated skin creases, not idealized skeletal joint centers, so a centroid-of-cluster measurement is the closer match, not a compromise).

`scaleFactor = caliperValue / (pooled middle-finger MCP→PIP + PIP→DIP + DIP→tip length)` is recomputed continuously as the pooled bone-length estimate updates through the session, rather than being locked from an early, noisier estimate, and is applied uniformly to every bone — valid because relative bone-length ratios across fingers are already trustworthy; only the single global scale is being corrected. A plausible numeric value (sanity range, e.g. 60–120mm for an adult middle finger) is required to complete; implausible values prompt re-measurement rather than a silent accept.

## Resting posture

Both hands, unsupported above the camera, settle into whatever tented angle feels natural for a relaxed typing-ready posture — no single prescribed orientation, because the point of this capture is exactly to find out where a given person's hands land, and that will fall somewhere in the palms-down-to-palms-facing-each-other range on its own. This is a genuine improvement over an earlier framing that asked for hands "resting flat on a desk": that posture is both a worse proxy for actual typing stance (fingers poised over keys, not passively splayed flat) and, from any camera placed near or above the desk, dorsal-facing — exactly the configuration to avoid. Held above a floor-facing-up camera instead, the natural relaxed posture stays inside the good orientation range by construction.

This capture is inherently **bilateral, and has to be** — the relative position and orientation between the two hands at rest (natural separation, mutual tenting angle) is itself a real design input, and it only exists in a frame where both hands are visible together; scanning each hand alone, even in a matching orientation, cannot recover it.

Recorded: per-frame joint angles for both hands throughout a several-second hold, plus each hand's own local basis (`makeBasis`) per frame. As with any hold, the user won't sit perfectly still and the first fraction of a second is typically still-settling, so a still-window search (frame-to-frame joint-angle velocity thresholding, discarding at least the first ~0.5s) picks the lowest-variance sub-window automatically, and neutral joint angles are averaged over it. The relative transform between the hands (separation distance, relative rotation) is computed over the same synchronized sub-window: separation distance is well-grounded even though absolute monocular scale is normally the weak point, because each hand's own caliper-corrected scale anchors real-world distance in the same shot — no fresh scale estimate needed. Completion requires a sub-window of minimum duration (e.g. 1s) where both hands simultaneously satisfy the velocity threshold; if none is found within the allotted hold (e.g. 5s), ask the user to hold more still, possibly lengthening the window.

More than one resting posture is possible and meaningfully different depending on what a design attempt is exploring (flat/low-tent, high-tent, "holding a ball," etc.) — gravity pulls the hands into a different equilibrium at zero voluntary effort depending on orientation, so there's no single universal "the" neutral pose. This capture can be repeated any number of times, independently of the rest of the session, under a free-text label a later placement/tenting UI can offer as an option.

## Flexion and extension range, bone length, and finger coupling

Palm facing the camera, one finger at a time in digit order (thumb → index → middle → ring → pinky). Instruction per finger: "move it to your comfortable maximum, without strain, and hold" (comfortable tier), then "now push it to your true limit, and hold" (full tier), each repeated three times. Palm-facing is preferred here primarily because it keeps all five fingers visibly separated with minimal mutual overlap, and MediaPipe's training data and depth cues are strongest in this orientation — not because it's the geometrically ideal angle for any single joint's flexion axis in isolation (a lateral/profile view is actually closer to ideal by that narrower measure, since it points the flexion rotation axis straight at the camera rather than across it). Occlusion-avoidance and MediaPipe's own reliability profile are treated as the deciding factors over idealized single-joint projective geometry; this is a judgment call worth checking against real capture data once it exists, not a settled result.

Recorded per frame: the active finger's MCP-Z, PIP-Z, and DIP-Z — DIP is retained even though it isn't independently instructed, since it's mechanically driven by PIP flexion and is needed for the coupling fit below. The comfortable hold gets the same still-window extraction as the resting-posture capture, giving a **comfortable-ROM bound** per joint. The full-tier sweep, with per-frame angles retained rather than pooled, gives **min/max = active full ROM** per joint, for MCP, PIP, and DIP together. The axis-fit confidence a joint's fit already produces internally (an SVD singular-value ratio) is surfaced here per joint rather than computed and discarded. **DIP-vs-PIP coupling** is fit directly — regress the retained DIP-Z against PIP-Z across the full-tier sweep, per finger, rather than assuming a literature ratio — since a hand can have a large DIP range on one finger and almost none on another; a near-zero fitted slope for a given finger is a valid result, not a failed fit.

**Bone length is harvested from this same capture, not from a dedicated rotation-arc phase.** With the hand held flat and palm-facing at the start of each finger's elicitation (before it begins flexing), each bone segment lies close to the frontoparallel plane relative to the fixed floor camera — near-ideal for length estimation, by construction of the prescribed geometry, without needing a separate slow-rotation instruction or a discovered calibration frame to know which frames were well-angled. Extended-hold frames from every finger's own elicitation, pooled across the session, replace what used to be a standalone bone-length phase.

Completion (plateau detection): track the running min and max angle for all three joints (MCP, PIP, DIP), not just the phase's nominal target — requiring DIP's own extremum to converge, not just PIP's, matters for a finger with a small-but-real DIP range, which could otherwise plateau on PIP well before DIP has been adequately sampled for the coupling fit. Declare convergence once the last two repetitions each fail to extend any of the three ranges by more than a small threshold (e.g. 2°); if the requested rep count finishes without convergence, prompt for more reps rather than accepting an unconverged bound.

**This capture is bilateral by default.** The instruction ("flex this finger to your maximum, then further") is simple, symmetric, and asks for no independent coordination between the two hands — mirroring it on both hands at once produces the same per-hand data (each hand's own joint angles and coupling fit are computed independently of whether the other hand happened to be in frame) in half the session time, with no coupling between hands to protect and no occlusion risk as long as they're held apart. MediaPipe already tracks two hands in one call, so nothing new is needed to support this.

**Flexion-plane paired-finger exploration** (for the enslaving matrix, described fully in the next section under abduction, since the two planes share a method) can be run in this same palm-facing orientation, for whichever finger pairs are of interest, immediately after the per-finger sweeps — also bilateral, for the same reason.

## Abduction range and cross-finger interaction

This is where a lateral view earns its place: with palms facing each other, an abduction rotation axis (roughly perpendicular to the palm, i.e. along the direction the palm faces) points close to the camera's own view direction instead of across it, and MediaPipe additionally isn't asked to resolve a face-on cluster of five fingers that abduction alone would otherwise crowd together in the image. Hands are unsupported (no desk to brace against), which also removes the earlier idea of pressing the other four fingers down for isolation — that bracing was there to prevent a full hard-press splay (a passive, proximally-driven phenomenon, mechanically distinct from active voluntary abduction, and explicitly not what this capture wants), not to solve a camera problem, and it's dropped rather than replaced: the same imperfect isolation this protocol already accepts for free-air motion elsewhere (below) applies here too.

Drag/block isolation and abduction range are captured together, as one freeform paired-motion trial per finger pair, rather than as separate phases — a single retained trajectory feeds every signal below, since true single-finger isolation without fixturing isn't realistic anyway (some cross-finger motion always leaks in) and a separately-instructed "hold at discrete targets" condition assumes a precision of static control that's no more achievable unfixtured than continuous motion is.

Instruction per pair (i, j): "move fingers i and j together, however feels natural — sometimes moving mostly one while relaxing the other, sometimes exploring both together, trying to cover as much of the range as you can, in as many combinations as you can, over about 20–30 seconds." Recorded: per-frame angle time series for both fingers, in whichever plane the trial targets (abduction-plane trials in this lateral orientation; flexion-plane trials, described above, in the palm-facing orientation). From the same trajectory:

- **Each finger's own abduction min/max** — the running extrema across the trial, exactly as if it were a dedicated solo sweep, since the freeform exploration necessarily visits each finger's own extremes on the way to covering the joint pair's full space. No separate solo-abduction elicitation is needed. Because the coverage grid below needs bounds before it can know which cells are "visited," and those bounds are usually anchored to an already-known ROM, this capture lets the grid's bounds grow with the observed extrema rather than being fixed in advance — a small adaptation from a design where ROM was always established before pairwise coverage was scored.
- **Enslaving coefficient** `E[i][j] ≈ Δθ_j/Δθ_i`, fit by regression over whichever sub-segments of the trajectory have i's excursion dominating j's — the "mostly one, relaxed other" portions of the freeform motion satisfy this without a separately-instructed condition, following the standard enslaving-matrix method ([PMC: origin of finger enslaving](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7814910/)), ported here from isometric force to unloaded kinematic angle, producing a personalized rather than literature-borrowed matrix.
- **Prohibited-region boundary**: bin the `(θ_i, θ_j)` space into a grid; wherever the trajectory, despite otherwise broad coverage, never visited a region, that gap is the measured constraint — geometric blocking and passive mechanical coupling both show up this way, with no finger-width guess needed. Apply a wider confidence margin here specifically, since tracking is weakest exactly at a blocking boundary (closely spaced, overlapping fingers).

Completion (coverage-grid detection): a minimum fraction of grid cells (e.g. 70%) need at least one confidence-passing sample. Live feedback should highlight unvisited cells. If coverage isn't met after the initial time-box, continue the same trial rather than restarting, with guidance toward the specific unfilled regions.

**Bilateral by default**, for the same reasoning as the flexion capture: the instruction is symmetric and simple enough to mirror on both hands at once with no added coordination burden, and each hand's coupling fit and coverage grid are independent of the other hand's presence in frame.

## Thumb carpometacarpal joint

The CMC is a saddle joint with two actively driven rotations (flexion-extension, abduction-adduction) and a third, conjunct axial rotation that falls out mechanically from the other two rather than being independently muscle-driven — specifically what makes true opposition (thumb pad rotating to face the fingertips) possible, rather than just the thumb swinging closer to the fingers. Treating this joint as fixed, as a simplified model does today, means the thumb's computed workspace is a cone anchored at one orientation, not a genuine opposition arc; getting this right is the main point of capturing the thumb at all. MediaPipe already tracks a landmark for this joint every frame — nothing about capturing it is a new visual capability, only fitting its axis and range from motion the way every other joint already is.

This is the one capture that stays single-hand, for two reasons that don't apply anywhere else in this protocol: the instruction (hold MCP/IP straight, move only from the base, "like swinging a stiff rod") asks for a self-monitored isolation that's a harder dual motor-control task to run correctly on both hands at once than a simple mirrored flex or freeform wiggle is; and the thumb, more than any other digit, is prone to self-occlusion against the palm or fingers, which triggers a reorientation response (below) that needs to be judged and acted on per hand — doing that for two hands simultaneously means tracking two independent retry states rather than one.

The outer sweep — thumb held rigid, swept through its full comfortable range from the CMC alone — is captured in the same two canonical orientations already established (palm-facing, then palms-facing-each-other-ish), rather than an arbitrary third angle, specifically because thumb self-occlusion is a known, not hypothetical, risk and a single fixed orientation isn't reliably going to keep the thumb clear of the palm and other fingers throughout a full sweep. Default to both orientations rather than waiting for a failure to trigger the switch. The CMC's flexion-extension and abduction-adduction range is fit via PCA/SVD on the observed motion — the same axis-fitting method used for every other joint, applied here for the first time to the CMC.

Plateau detection applies as elsewhere, with one addition: plateau detection alone can't distinguish "the joint genuinely stopped" from "confidence collapsed as the thumb rotated out of view, silently truncating the sweep," so a plateau is only accepted if confidence/frame-yield stayed healthy right up to the apparent extreme. If yield was dropping as the extremum was approached, that's occlusion-truncation, not a true limit — retry in the other of the two orientations, not with more repetitions in the same one; more time won't reveal a region the current orientation structurally can't see.

A freeform sweep follows, unconstrained, moving the thumb through as much of its reachable space as feels natural, in both orientations as needed for the same occlusion reasons. From this trajectory: **conjunct-rotation coupling**, regressing the observed axial twist against the two driven angles (flexion, abduction) — the CMC analogue of `E[i][j]`, fit within one joint's three rotations rather than across two fingers, and not elicited as its own instruction since it isn't independently controllable; and **thumb MCP/IP flexion range**, as a byproduct of the same trajectory, on the premise that a natural comfortable movement will appear in freeform coverage without a separate scripted per-joint thumb test — worth validating once real capture data exists, since this trajectory is a superposition of everything moving at once, a harder per-joint extraction problem than the other fingers' isolated single-joint sweeps. Completion bins the observed data into named regions (near each fingertip, near the top of the index finger, and whatever else emerges as meaningful once real sessions are run) rather than a fixed grid; a region unfilled despite continued time in the current orientation triggers the same re-orient-and-continue escalation as the outer sweep, building on the same retained coverage map rather than restarting.

## Completion-detection patterns, generalized

Three reusable patterns cover everything above:

- **Plateau detection** (flexion/extension full tier, thumb outer sweep): track a running extremum across repeated cycles; declare convergence once the last k cycles each fail to extend it by more than a small threshold. The thumb's occlusion-truncation guard layers a confidence/yield check on top of this base pattern — worth considering for any other capture where occlusion risk turns out to be non-trivial in practice, not only the thumb.
- **Coverage-grid detection** (paired finger sweeps, thumb freeform sweep): bin a 2D or named-region space into cells; declare convergence once a minimum fraction has been visited with confidence-passing data. Used whenever the target is a region rather than a single bound, so exhaustive duration isn't itself the goal.
- **Still-window detection** (resting posture, comfortable-tier holds): low-variance-window extraction over a freeform hold, via frame-to-frame velocity thresholding.

Every capture additionally fails, independent of its own criterion, if accepted-frame yield after confidence-filtering is too low — the one universal retry trigger under all three patterns.

## Wrist and forearm: out of scope

No fixture constrains a forearm well enough to measure wrist articulation directly — pinning one bony point against a desk still leaves the forearm free to rotate around it, and preventing that would require bone-only contact at both the wrist and elbow simultaneously, which is an external fixator, not a desk setup. MediaPipe Hands also has no elbow/forearm landmark, so even a well-constrained forearm would only let wrist angle be inferred indirectly. Wrist/forearm posture is a manual placement decision made in the design tool (via `SolvedHand.position`, already a free 6-DOF anchor), informed by wrist-posture literature as live guardrail feedback during that placement, not something this capture protocol measures.

## Open items, deliberately left unresolved here

- **Whether palm-facing is really the better choice for flexion capture, versus a lateral view that's closer to geometrically ideal for that joint's rotation axis specifically** — argued above on occlusion/training-data grounds, not derived from first-hand capture data. Worth a direct before/after comparison once real sessions exist.
- **DIP-vs-PIP coupling's functional form** (a simple linear ratio vs. something with an offset/dead-zone/nonlinearity near the extremes) is fit, not assumed, but the choice of a linear fit itself isn't validated — worth checking once per-finger data exists, especially for fingers with a very small DIP range where noise could dominate a near-flat fit.
- **The thumb's occlusion-triggered reorientation logic and the two-orientation default are new, untested design choices** — worth checking against real capture data whether they're sufficient, not assumed correct because the reasoning is sound.
- **Whether the freeform thumb trajectory yields clean enough per-joint MCP/IP angle data** is an assumption, flagged inline above, repeated here since it's a real risk to skipping a dedicated thumb flexion test.
- **Whether a genuine second (harder-but-still-active) abduction tier exists at all** is left as "probably not, a single tier is honest," not conclusively ruled out.
- **Bimanual/interlimb coupling** — capturing both hands simultaneously for every phase above (a change from treating one-hand-at-a-time as the default) assumes mirrored bilateral motion doesn't meaningfully corrupt either hand's own per-joint measurements. This hasn't been empirically tested. If early data suggests cross-hand interference, the fallback is doing the affected capture one hand at a time, at the cost of session length.
- **Coverage-grid resolution** (how coarse the bins can be before a prohibited-region boundary becomes useless) needs empirical tuning.
- **`E[i][j]` is measured from slow, deliberate exploration, and is assumed — not validated — to also hold during fast, real-keystroke-speed motion.** Accepted as the only practical option absent a fast-tapping capture trial; this assumption is load-bearing for any accidental-activation check that reads it, worth revisiting with an actual fast-tapping trial if those predictions prove unreliable in practice.
